What is AI, and how did it change the world?

Before you use AI at work, it helps to know what it is, where it came from, how much money is behind it, and how real organizations already use it, including banks and telecoms in Tanzania and Kenya.

In this chapter you will learn

  • A plain definition of AI, machine learning and generative AI
  • Seventy years of AI history, from a 1950 question to a billion weekly users
  • How big the AI economy has become, in spending and company value
  • How businesses in Africa and around the world already use AI, and what went wrong for some

1. A plain definition

Artificial intelligence (AI) is computer software that does tasks we normally associate with human thinking: recognizing faces and voices, understanding language, spotting patterns, making predictions and, more recently, writing and drawing.

Older software follows rules a programmer writes by hand: “if the balance is below zero, block the payment.” Most modern AI instead learns patterns from examples. Show it millions of past transactions labelled “fraud” or “not fraud”, and it learns to flag new suspicious ones by itself. This is called machine learning.

2. AI you already use every day

Mobile money

Safaricom has built AI into M-Pesa for real-time monitoring and fraud detection [W6].

Customer chatbots

Safaricom's Zuri, CRDB Bank's Elle and NMB Bank's Jirani answer customers on WhatsApp and online [W6][W7][W8].

Your phone

Face unlock, voice typing, spam filters, map traffic times and photo search all use machine learning.

4. The money behind AI

AI is now one of the largest investments in economic history. Most of the money so far goes into chips, data centres and cloud services, built by technology companies.

5. How businesses run with AI

Four real organizations, three of them African, show what AI does in practice, and what goes wrong.

Case 1

Klarna (Sweden, payments): AI customer service, then a correction

1
What they did

In its first month (2024), Klarna's AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of all customer service chats, which Klarna said equalled the work of 700 full-time agents [W10]. Resolution time reportedly fell from 11 minutes to under 2 [W10].

2
What went wrong

In May 2025 Klarna reversed parts of its AI-only approach and began hiring human agents again [W10].

3
The lesson for NGOs

AI handled the high volume of simple, repetitive questions; harder cases still needed people. The same balance applies to any helpline or beneficiary feedback line.

Case 2

Safaricom (Kenya, telecoms and M-Pesa): the Zuri chatbot

1
What they did

Safaricom launched Zuri in 2018 on Telegram and Facebook Messenger to handle tasks like M-Pesa reversals, balance checks and airtime top-ups, and later added WhatsApp [W6]. Safaricom has since built generative AI and fraud detection into M-Pesa [W6], and in 2025 launched FarmerAI with the NGO Opportunity International to advise smallholder farmers through its DigiFarm service [W6].

2
What went wrong

In 2026 a customer took a case to Kenya's High Court arguing that Zuri cannot adequately resolve all customer concerns; the article noted Kenya has no specific law regulating AI [W6].

3
The lesson for NGOs

People must always be able to reach a human. A chatbot that blocks that path damages trust, however efficient it is.

Case 3

CRDB Bank and NMB Bank (Tanzania): AI in everyday banking

1
What they did

CRDB Bank launched “Elle”, an AI customer service chatbot, in October 2024 [W7], and rebuilt its SimBanking app using AI [W7]. NMB Bank introduced “Jirani”, an AI chatbot on WhatsApp that lets customers buy motor insurance from their phones [W8]; more than 4.7 million customers use NMB Mkononi, and 96% of the bank's transactions in 2024 were digital [W8].

2
The lesson for NGOs

Tanzanians already talk to AI in Swahili on WhatsApp. Your beneficiaries and members are getting used to it, so NGOs need staff who understand both its value and its limits.

Case 4

Nvidia (USA, chips): selling the tools behind the boom

1
What they did

Nvidia makes the graphics chips (GPUs) used to train and run most AI models. In October 2025 it became the first public company ever valued at $5 trillion [W5].

2
The lesson for NGOs

Most of today's AI money flows to the companies that build the infrastructure. For NGOs, the opportunity is different: powerful tools that once cost millions are now available on a phone, often free.

6. What this means for your organization

  1. The tools are already in your office. Staff are using them, with or without rules.
  2. Big companies learned the hard way that AI works best on high-volume, repetitive work, with people handling the hard cases.
  3. Spending is not the same as value. Most organizations are still piloting. Measure what AI saves you, honestly.
  4. Trust is the asset NGOs cannot lose. Use AI in ways that protect the people you serve. The rest of this course shows how.

Key points

  • AI is software that learns patterns from examples; generative AI creates new text and images.
  • The field is 70 years old, but the modern boom began with deep learning in 2012 and ChatGPT in 2022.
  • Worldwide AI spending is forecast at about $2.67 trillion in 2026, mostly on infrastructure.
  • Businesses from Klarna to CRDB Bank use AI for high-volume customer work, and the best keep humans available.
  • Figures in this chapter are dated; check the sources for the latest numbers.

Chapter 1 of 4

Understanding AI

AI writing tools are fast drafting assistants that sound certain even when they are wrong. You remain the author of everything you send.

Step 1: See what the tool is actually doing

AI writing tools are built on large language models. They learned patterns from enormous amounts of text, and they write by predicting the most likely next word, again and again. They do not open a file or check a database before they answer.

Step 2: Know where it helps and where it fails

Good at

  • First drafts of emails, reports and minutes
  • Summarizing documents you paste in
  • Rewording for a new audience or reading level
  • Turning messy notes into structure
  • First-draft translations

Weak at

  • Your organization's facts and figures
  • Exact numbers, dates and names
  • Recent events and local context
  • Real quotations and real sources
  • Judgments about people

Step 3: Recognize a hallucination

A hallucination is a confident answer that is false. It looks exactly like a real answer, often with a source attached.

Real instance

Amina asks for a statistic for a funding proposal

1
What Amina typed
What percentage of NGOs in Tanzania use AI tools? Give a source.
2
The kind of answer she got (illustrative)
According to a 2024 national survey by the Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority, 47% of registered NGOs in Tanzania use AI tools, mainly for communications and reporting.
3
What checking revealed
  • Amina searched the regulator's website: no such survey exists.
  • When we researched this guide, we found no published national figure for AI use by Tanzanian NGOs at all.
  • The number, the year and the source were all invented, and they sounded perfectly plausible.
4
What she used instead
A 2025 global survey of 2,539 humanitarian workers found that 93% had used AI tools, but only 22% worked under a formal AI policy (Humanitarian Leadership Academy and Data Friendly Space).

A real figure from a source she opened and read herself.

Try it now · 10 minutes
  1. Paste a short, non-confidential piece of your own work into your AI tool and ask: “Summarize this in five bullet points.”
  2. Compare the summary with the original. Mark anything wrong or missing.
  3. Ask a question it cannot know: “How many people did my organization reach last year?” Does it admit it doesn't know, or invent a number?

Step 4: Avoid the five common mistakes

  1. Believing the first answer without checking.
  2. Pasting confidential information into a public tool.
  3. Asking vague questions and expecting precise answers.
  4. Sending AI text without reading it.
  5. Using AI output in donor documents without checking the donor's rules.

The three habits

Give the facts yourself. Read everything before you send it. Keep red data out.

Chapter 2 of 4

Prompting for NGO tasks

A prompt is your instruction to the tool. Specific prompts that contain your own facts get usable drafts; one-line requests get generic text with invented details.

Step 1: Build every prompt from four parts

Step 2: See the difference it makes

Worked example

Amina writes a post about the reading club

1
Weak prompt and its result
Prompt
Write a post about our reading club.
Output (illustrative)
Our reading club is transforming lives! Hundreds of girls across Tanzania are discovering the joy of books, and their grades have doubled. Join us in changing the future!
  • “Hundreds of girls” and “grades have doubled” are invented. The club has 40 learners and measured reading speed, not grades.
  • Posting this would mislead the public and the donor.
2
Strong prompt and its result
Prompt
You are the communications lead of a girls' education NGO in Dar es Salaam. Write a Facebook post about our reading club results. Facts: 40 learners in Standards 4 to 6 at one partner primary school; 12 weeks of after-school sessions; average reading speed rose from 38 to 52 words per minute. Under 80 words, warm tone, no names or photos of children, end by inviting volunteers. Use only these facts.
Output (illustrative)
Twelve weeks, 40 young readers, real progress. 📚 Learners in Standards 4 to 6 at our partner school raised their average reading speed from 38 to 52 words per minute in our after-school reading club. That is more confidence in every lesson. Want to help the next group grow? Volunteer as a reading buddy: message us to join.
  • Every fact matches what Amina supplied.
  • She still checks it: the emoji is fine for Facebook, the numbers are correct, no child is identifiable. It is ready to post.

Step 3: Add two safety instructions

Mark gaps

“If something is missing, write [MISSING] instead of guessing.” Invisible guesses become visible gaps you fill.

Ask first

“Ask me up to five questions before you write.” The tool tells you what it needs instead of inventing it.

Chapter 3 of 4

Checking and protecting

Two habits prevent almost every serious AI mistake at work: sort information by risk before you paste it, and run four checks on everything the tool gives back.

Step 1: Sort by the traffic light

Worked example

Neema wants AI help with a field case summary

1
The original field note: red
Halima Said, 34, from Igoma ward, HIV-positive, missed two antenatal visits. She says transport costs TZS 6,000 each way and her husband controls the money.

Highlighted: details that identify her or are sensitive health data. All names are fictional.

2
Removing the name is not enough

“A 34-year-old HIV-positive woman in Igoma ward” could still identify one person in a small community. HIV status is sensitive health data that never goes into a public tool, with or without a name.

3
The rewritten version: amber, usable if policy allows
Several women in one of our project wards missed antenatal visits. They report that transport costs are high and that they do not control household money.

Now it describes a pattern, not a person. Neema can ask AI to help her draft a donor paragraph on transport barriers.

Game: Red, amber or green?

Classify each item. Feedback appears as you choose.

Step 2: Run the four checks on everything

1 · Facts

Is every claim true and in your records?

2 · Numbers

Are figures, dates and percentages right? Recalculate.

3 · Names and quotes

Are they real, correct and permitted?

4 · Wording and bias

Is it fair and respectful to the community?

Worked example

Neema checks an AI-drafted donor paragraph

1
Her facts
Quarter 3: 412 antenatal clients registered on SMS reminders; 286 attended 4 or more visits; 2 health centres; no client quotes collected.
2
AI draft with the four checks applied
In Quarter 3, the project registered 412 clients for SMS reminders, of whom 286 (75%) attended four or more visits across three health centres. As one mother said, “The reminders saved my baby's life.” These results show that rural mothers are often careless about clinic visits without outside support.
  • Numbers: 286 of 412 is 69.4%, not 75%.
  • Facts: two health centres, not three.
  • Quotes: no quotes were collected. Invented, so it is removed.
  • Wording and bias: “careless” blames mothers; the barrier is transport and cost.
3
Final paragraph
In Quarter 3, the project registered 412 clients for SMS reminders, of whom 286 (69%) attended four or more antenatal visits across two health centres. Clients continue to report transport costs as the main barrier to attendance.

Game: Spot the errors

The source facts are true. Click every phrase in the AI draft that is wrong, then press Check.

Source facts

  • Kijani Community Network (fictional)
  • Water-safety workshop on 12 March
  • 48 participants: 30 women, 18 men
  • Held in three villages
  • No participant was quoted in the field notes

AI draft

On 21 March, Kijani Community Network held a water-safety workshop for 48 participants across five villages. Seventy-five percent of participants were women. One participant said, “This workshop changed how my family drinks water every day.” The workshop proves that clean water habits spread quickly in rural communities.

Step 3: Consent and donor rules

  • Get consent before using anyone's story, photo or details, with or without AI.
  • Check each donor's rules on AI use before using AI in funded work.
  • Record when AI helped draft a document if your organization or donor requires it.

Two kinds of AI in this chapter

Everyday AI tools (chat assistants) are what you will use: drafting, summarizing, translating. Purpose-built AI, such as a maternal health SMS service or a crop disease app, is built by specialist teams over years. Those examples show what is possible and what made it work.

Published evidence means a sourced example with reported results. Practical uses means we found no strong published evaluation, so treat the ideas as pilots to test and measure.

Toolkit

Safety and data rules

When in doubt, leave it out. Keep this page where you work.

Before you paste anything, ask

  1. Could this identify a beneficiary, a case or a staff member?
  2. Is this confidential to my organization or a donor?
  3. Do my organization's or donor's rules forbid AI for this task?

If the answer to any question is yes or unsure, do not paste it.

The seven rules

1Never paste beneficiary names, case details or safeguarding information into public AI tools.
2Never paste confidential donor, financial or staff data into public AI tools.
3Check every number, name and quote AI produces before using it.
4Get consent before using anyone's story, photo or details, with or without AI.
5Check each donor's rules on AI use before using AI in funded work.
6Watch for biased or stereotyped wording about the communities you serve.
7Follow Tanzania's Personal Data Protection Act and your organization's policy; ask when unsure.

Research with AI safely

  • AI can invent sources, quotes and statistics that look real (see Amina's example in Chapter 1).
  • Ask for links, then open every one. Confirm the page exists and says what the AI claims.
  • Prefer official sources: donors, government, regulators, UN agencies, universities.
  • Ask of every source: Who wrote it? When? What evidence? Who benefits?

If something goes wrong

If you paste something you should not have, stop. Do not paste more. Tell your supervisor or data protection contact right away, following your organization's policy.

Glossary and sources

Glossary

AI tool / assistant
Software that drafts, summarizes and answers questions in plain language, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot or Gemini.
Large language model
The technology behind AI writing tools: it predicts likely text from patterns learned from huge amounts of writing.
Prompt
Your instruction to an AI tool. Strong prompts have a role, task, context and format.
Hallucination
A confident answer that is false, such as an invented statistic, survey or quote.
Bias
Unfair or stereotyped patterns in AI output, often learned from its training data.
Personal data
Any information that can identify a person, directly or combined with other details.
Anonymization
Removing details so no one can be identified. Removing names alone is often not enough.
Shadow AI
Staff using AI tools at work without organizational approval, policy or oversight.
Purpose-built AI
AI designed for one task, such as diagnosing crop disease from a leaf photo.
